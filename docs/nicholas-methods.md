# Method Selection: A Numerical Approach

## 1. Purpose of the paper

This paper presents a numerical process for selecting a mining method, with emphasis on underground mass-mining techniques such as caving, induced caving, and stoping.

The main idea is to move away from method selection based only on experience and district practice, and toward a structured evaluation of measurable deposit characteristics. The paper argues that early design decisions should be based on the deposit's geometry, grade distribution, and rock mechanics, because small differences in these characteristics can materially change the most suitable mining method.

## 2. Core selection philosophy

The paper frames method selection as a two-stage process:

1. Stage 1: describe the deposit and rank the methods that are technically compatible.
2. Stage 2: cost the most likely methods in a general mine plan and compare their economic performance.

Stage 1 is intended to reduce a long list of possible methods to a short list of practical candidates. Stage 2 is intended to estimate cut-off grade, minable reserve, and economic feasibility.

## 3. Parameters that control selection

The paper identifies seven broad groups of inputs:

- Geometry and grade distribution of the deposit.
- Rock mass strength for the ore zone, hanging wall, and footwall.
- Mining costs and capitalization requirements.
- Mining rate.
- Type and availability of labor.
- Environmental concerns.
- Other site-specific constraints.

The first two groups, plus costs, are treated as the most influential for method selection.

## 4. Data required at the start

The paper emphasizes that the most important information for selection and initial layout is:

- Geologic sections.
- Level maps.
- A grade model.
- Rock mechanics characteristics for the ore, hanging wall, and footwall.

A major warning in the paper is that much of this information must be captured during drilling and core logging. If it is not collected early, it may be lost.

### 4.1 Geology and mapping

Recommended outputs include interpreted geologic sections and level plans that show:

- rock types,
- alteration zones,
- faults,
- veins,
- fold axes,
- and other major structures.

These maps should be prepared at the same scale as mine planning drawings, and sections should be drawn at true scale without vertical exaggeration.

The paper also recommends extending mapping beyond the ore limits so there is enough information for assessing surface movement and placing shafts, adits, and buildings.

### 4.2 Grade model

The grade model should be plotted on the same scale as the geology maps. It can be contoured by grade or displayed as colored grade blocks. When overlain on the geology sections, it helps identify the spatial relationship between ore and rock units.

## 5. Deposit geometry and grade distribution

The paper defines geometry in four ways:

- General shape.
- Ore thickness.
- Plunge.
- Depth below surface.

Grade distribution is classified as:

- Uniform.
- Gradational.
- Erratic.

### 5.1 General shape

The paper uses three broad shape classes:

- Equi-dimensional: all dimensions are of similar order.
- Tabular or platy: two dimensions are much larger than thickness.
- Irregular: dimensions vary significantly over short distances.

### 5.2 Ore thickness

Thickness is grouped as:

- Narrow: less than 10 m.
- Intermediate: 10 to 30 m.
- Thick: 30 to 100 m.
- Very thick: greater than 100 m.

### 5.3 Plunge

Plunge is grouped as:

- Flat: less than 20 degrees.
- Intermediate: 20 to 55 degrees.
- Steep: greater than 55 degrees.

### 5.4 Grade distribution

- Uniform: grade does not vary much from the mean.
- Gradational: grade changes gradually in zones.
- Erratic: grade changes rapidly and irregularly over short distances.

## 6. Rock mechanics characterization for Stage 1

The paper uses three basic rock mechanics descriptors:

- Rock substance strength.
- Fracture spacing.
- Fracture shear strength.

These are evaluated separately for:

- the ore zone,
- the hanging wall,
- and the footwall.

The paper notes that several established rock mass classification systems can support this work, but it prefers a simple ranking framework for selection.

### 6.1 Rock substance strength

Rock substance strength is treated as the ratio of intact uniaxial compressive strength to overburden pressure.

The paper groups this ratio as:

- Weak: less than 8.
- Moderate: 8 to 15.
- Strong: greater than 15.

### 6.2 Fracture spacing

Fracture spacing can be defined either as fractures per meter or by RQD. The paper prefers fractures per meter because it is more quantitative for fragment size.

The broad classes used are:

- Very close.
- Close.
- Wide.
- Very wide.

### 6.3 Fracture shear strength

Fracture shear strength is classified qualitatively as:

- Weak: clean smooth joint or weak infill.
- Moderate: clean rough joint.
- Strong: infill stronger than the rock substance.

## 7. Mining methods considered

The paper evaluates ten mining methods:

1. Open pit.
2. Block caving.
3. Sublevel stoping.
4. Sublevel caving.
5. Longwall.
6. Room-and-pillar.
7. Shrinkage stoping.
8. Cut-and-fill.
9. Top slicing.
10. Square-set.

These are not treated as equally applicable. Each method is ranked against the deposit characteristics.

## 8. Ranking logic used in Stage 1

The paper assigns each method a compatibility rank for the geometry/grade categories and for each rock mechanics category in the ore zone, hanging wall, and footwall.

The ranks are:

- Preferred: the characteristic is favorable.
- Probable: the method can work if the characteristic exists.
- Unlikely: the method is not helped, but not fully ruled out.
- Eliminated: the characteristic makes the method unusable.

The paper converts these ranks into numeric values so that the values can be added. The intent is that any eliminated condition drives the overall total negative and removes the method from consideration.

### 8.1 Meaning of the numeric values

The paper uses numeric scores approximately as follows:

- Preferred: 3 to 4.
- Probable: 1 to 2.
- Unlikely: 0.
- Eliminated: -49.

The large negative elimination value is deliberate. It forces incompatible methods out of the ranking.

## 9. Geometry and grade distribution ranking

The paper ranks each mining method against the geometry and grade classes in a detailed table.

The key outcome is that geometry and grade distribution already separate the methods into broad families:

- Open pit is favored by broad, favorable geometry and near-surface conditions.
- Block caving, sublevel caving, and top slicing may rank well for very thick or massive deposits depending on geology and rock behavior.
- Cut-and-fill and square-set tend to remain viable where geometry is less favorable but support is needed.
- Room-and-pillar and longwall can be strongly penalized when the shape and plunge do not fit.

## 10. Rock mechanics ranking by zone

The paper applies separate rankings for the ore zone, hanging wall, and footwall.

### 10.1 Ore zone

This table reflects whether the ore itself can support the selected method. Stronger ore and favorable fracture conditions tend to support open pit and some caving methods. Weak or unstable ore can favor supported stoping methods.

### 10.2 Hanging wall

The hanging wall is critical because many methods fail if the overlying rock cannot remain stable long enough, or cannot cave in a controlled way when caving is required.

Methods such as sublevel caving and block caving depend strongly on whether the hanging wall is likely to cave appropriately.

### 10.3 Footwall

The footwall affects drift stability, support requirements, and in some cases pillar behavior. It can change the feasibility of stoping and caving methods, and it can also influence open pit slope behavior through overall rock mass competence.

## 11. Example of Stage 1 total ranking

The paper gives a worked example in which the deposit has the following characteristics:

- Tabular or platy general shape.
- Very thick ore.
- Flat plunge.
- Uniform grade distribution.
- Depth around 130 m.
- Moderate ore-zone rock substance strength.
- Close fracture spacing in the ore.
- Moderate fracture strength in the ore.
- Strong hanging wall rock substance.
- Wide fracture spacing in the hanging wall.
- Moderate hanging-wall fracture strength.
- Moderate footwall rock substance.
- Close footwall fracture spacing.
- Weak footwall fracture strength.

The paper then sums the scores by category and ranks the methods.

### 11.1 Example totals

The example produces the following overall ranking:

- Open pit: 40.
- Block caving: 34.
- Top slicing: 34.
- Square-set: 33.
- Cut-and-fill: 32.
- Shrinkage stoping: 30.
- Sublevel caving: 29.
- Sublevel stoping: 24.
- Room-and-pillar: -20.
- Longwall: -18.

The paper highlights that open pit is the obvious choice in the example from a geometry and rock mechanics point of view, while several underground methods remain close enough to deserve further comparison.

## 12. Interpretation of the ranking tables

The paper makes an important point: the choice of mining method is a compromise.

For example:

- Cut-and-fill may perform well on rock mechanics but poorly on geometry.
- Top slicing may perform well on geometry but poorly on rock mechanics.

Because the rankings differ by characteristic group, the user can see which aspect of the deposit is helping or hurting a method.

## 13. How the paper uses cost at the end of Stage 1

After Stage 1 ranking, the paper recommends narrowing the list to the few highest-ranked methods and then comparing them by general mining cost.

The paper gives a simplified increasing-cost ordering of methods, showing that:

- Open pit and block caving are among the lower-cost options.
- Cut-and-fill, top slicing, and square-set are among the higher-cost options.

The point is not that the cheapest method always wins. The point is that cost should only be examined after technical filtering.

## 14. Stage 2: detailed mine planning inputs

Stage 2 is the engineering and economic refinement stage. The paper says that the selected short list should be carried into a more detailed mine plan so that cut-off grade and minable reserves can be estimated.

The paper then explains the additional data needed for Stage 2:

- Uniaxial compressive strength.
- Young's modulus.
- Poisson's ratio.
- Tensile strength.
- Intact rock shear strength.
- Natural fracture shear strength.
- Fault gouge shear strength.
- Creep tests for certain rock types such as salt or shale.

The paper notes that unsplit drill core should be preserved for these tests.

## 15. Geologic structure in Stage 2

The paper distinguishes between two structure types:

- Major structures: faults, folds, dikes, and other large-scale features.
- Rock fabric: more frequent joints and discontinuities.

Structural data can be gathered through:

- detail line mapping,
- cell mapping,
- oriented core mapping.

### 15.1 Detail line mapping

This measures fracture characteristics along a line and gives a joint-by-joint description of a structural domain.

### 15.2 Cell mapping

This measures mean orientation and fracture characteristics within a spatial cell and helps delineate structural domains.

### 15.3 Oriented core

Oriented core provides structural information where outcrop data are not available, though it does not provide joint length.

## 16. Pre-mine stress and hydrology

The paper treats pre-mine stress as difficult to measure directly. It suggests using geologic history, structure orientation, and fault movement to estimate the stress field when direct methods are too expensive.

Hydrology is also important because it affects both rock strength and mining cost. The paper recommends considering:

- water table position,
- water sources,
- water-bearing structures,
- and pump tests.

## 17. Stage 2 implications by mining method

The paper then explains what Stage 2 rock mechanics information is needed for each major method family.

### 17.1 Open pit

For open pit, the key decision is whether it is better to remain in open pit or transition underground. The paper links this to mineral value, stripping ratio, and slope angle.

Slope angle should be estimated from geologic structure and stability, not assumed at a generic 45 degrees.

### 17.2 Block caving

For block caving, the main Stage 2 questions are:

- Is the deposit cavable?
- What is the minimum drawpoint spacing?
- What supportable drift size is required?
- What is the subsidence limit?

The paper describes cavability in terms of fragment size distribution and undercut width. It cites the use of fragment-size analysis, RQD-based methods, and geomechanical classification schemes.

The paper also discusses the pressure arch concept and the importance of undercut width relative to the maximum load-transfer distance.

### 17.3 Drawpoint spacing and drift size

For caving methods, drawpoint spacing is influenced by fragment size, dilution control, and pillar strength. The paper notes that the correlation between fragment size and draw width is not always strong, so pillar analysis is also needed.

Gathering drift size should be estimated using support requirements and load-carrying capacity.

### 17.4 Stoping methods

For sublevel stoping and shrinkage stoping, the two main design variables are:

- stope width,
- pillar size.

The paper uses pressure-arch logic for stope width and pillar analysis for load capacity.

### 17.5 Sublevel caving

For sublevel caving, the paper says the hanging wall must cave behind the ore zone. If it does not, the method is not suitable.

The method also requires estimates for:

- cavability of the hanging wall,
- drift size,
- support,
- drift spacing,
- sublevel interval,
- and pillar stability.

## 18. Concluding message of the paper

The paper concludes that mining method selection should be based primarily on:

- geometry and grade distribution,
- rock mechanics of the ore zone, hanging wall, and footwall,
- and mining/capitalization cost.

The author stresses that rock mechanics should receive first priority in the decision process.

The final recommendation is to:

- define the deposit in Stage 1,
- rank the candidate methods,
- then develop detailed mine plans for the two or three highest-ranked options in Stage 2.

## 19. Practical takeaway for the app

For the application, this means the user should treat the output as a technical shortlist. The app is not trying to replace engineering judgment. It is trying to make the first screening step faster, more consistent, and easier to defend.

## 20. Key terminology used in the paper

- Open pit: surface mining with waste removal to expose ore.
- Block caving: undercutting a rock mass so it caves under its own weight.
- Sublevel stoping: ore is blasted from sublevels and drawn out.
- Sublevel caving: ore is blasted and the hanging wall caves with extraction.
- Longwall: continuous mining along a long face with support and cave behavior.
- Room-and-pillar: rooms are mined while pillars remain to support the roof.
- Shrinkage stoping: broken ore is left in the stope until mining is complete.
- Cut-and-fill: each slice is mined and then replaced with fill.
- Top slicing: horizontal lifts are mined in sequence with a timber mat.
- Square-set: timber sets are built to support mined openings.

## 22. Compact summary

This paper proposes a numerical mining method selection workflow that first filters methods by geology and rock mechanics, then checks the economics of the few best-ranked options. The central contribution is a structured ranking approach that makes the selection process more transparent and repeatable.
